### How do you find fulfilment in a career which no longer resembles what you signed up for? 

These days, I keep coming across fellow engineers on the internet who say things to the effect of "AI has taken the joy out of software development" or "My work is less meaningful now that I no longer write code". I didn't realize how much I had been experiencing this feeling until I heard it reflected by others. 

I find that coding tools are at a point where they are roughly as good as most programmers at the actual task of writing programs. That's not to make the claim that is so often shouted by victims of the AI hype cycle: software developers are out of a job. Notwithstanding the fact that I say this as a software developer myself, the job isn't going away. Instead the job function is changing to focus on higher level, artictectural work, and less on typing characters in a code editor - and I won't pretend I'm the first to come up with this.

My point is that most software developers write much less of their own code than they used to, and it's increasingly necessary to leverage AI tools to be competitive in the industry. Although AI doesn't write all the code for projects I create, a majority of my time is spent reading, understanding, and reasoning about AI generated code, file comments, and explanations. Aside from the mental labor of having to read the insufferable tone of AI-code-speak, it's also just not what I signed up for.

#### I remember vividly what got me hooked on programming at the start.

Perhaps because it came naturally to me, I fell in love with the act of dissecting a computer-related problem, understanding the fundamental logic that the problem required to solve, and implementing that logic using predictable but versatile tools in Python. 

My first computer science course was one of my favorite I ever took. The department of computer science at William & Mary was very tactical in this regard - first semester is all fun and games, solve the problem in whatever way you like! What even is time complexity anyway? By the time you're hooked, the next three semesters are data structures, algorithms, and software development. I wonder if this was deliberate.

Recently this lower-level, intimate relationship with the actual logic you author is something I rarely enjoy. And whenever I do, I can't help the nagging feeling that an AI would have already completed the task by now and I could be on to the next ticket. In the span of less than four years, since I began studying computer science to when I graduated, AI went from a primitive party trick to the singular defining tool used to write software. 

#### I find myself in the unique position of having studied computer science both before and after the broad adoption of AI coding tools.

Like many young people in this position, I felt somewhat cheated. It was too late to change my major, but it was starting to seem like I'd be out of a job before I even got one. However, I started to understand something that I hope the industry will soon realize as well - we still need software developers, they just have to know how to use AI.

So maybe I wasn't out of a job just yet, as long as the market eventually stabilized and started valuing the price of AI at what it actually costs - but that's another topic. Yes, junior developer roles are legitimately scarce and more competitive than ever, but the real bummer for me became the experience I alluded to earlier. Even if I got a job, I wouldn't be writing much code myself. 

The pressure to use AI tools didn't go away once I got home from class. When doing side projects, I would use coding tools for tasks I knew I could do myself, just less quickly. I found that while working on personal projects, my workflow shrunk to little more than a natural language conversation and decision oversight process. I felt like a senior engineer except I lacked the actual experience of one, and worse, I wasn't even managing a real human with a personality. 

#### The excitement of being able to easily create anything I imagined melted away as soon as I got used to how dreary the process actually was.

This is where I found myself after graduating, in need of a very scarce job very soon, that once I actually landed, would no longer ask me to do the work I loved. The prospect weighed on me like a quarter life crisis that I could see unfolding all too clearly. The problem that made this situation so tricky is that doing the work I actually wanted to do - writing code, coming up with little algorithms and and logic to solve problems - represented a very real opportunity cost in terms of productivity. It was impossible to find joy in doing these things when I knew I could be getting more done faster by just prompting it.

It turns out there's no perfect solution to the problem. Just like so many things in software. That nagging feeling I was experiencing is technically correct, it is less efficient to write my own code, and to some extent it will be the same quality output. But there's another benefit to actually writing your own code. Well, really two.

On some level you have to practice writing code in order to know what's going on. Those who enjoyed years of their career doing manual programming before AI messed it all up, already have lots of this practice. I was not so fortunate. Even though much of software development is now prompting and understanding responses, some fundamental sense of best practice, code smell, and architectural intuition is necessary to be successful. Writing code is a great way to stretch the mental muscles that underpin these skills.

More importantly, it's actually fun. It's kind of like exercise, I sometimes I had to force myself to exit Claude code, poke through the code editor, and sit with the discomfort of not knowing something. But once I did it, I found coding much more rewarding again.

#### It felt like I had rediscovered why I was doing all this in the first place.

So what's the lesson I learned, why should you care if you're a developer? You should care because this idea is a legitimate way to mitigate AI-burnout, and it's so simple. Just turn off Claude from time to time. 

I'm not saying retire these tools completely, that's probably a bad idea. I still use AI to help with most of the work I do, but I now have a technique in my back pocket for when I get sick of it. I know it might be uncommon to hear in the world of tech, but seriously, dust off the cobwebs and turn on your brain for an afternoon. For old times' sake.
