#### Play the game [here](https://hollowknightsilksong.com/) before reading! It's well worth it.

Take a second to think back to a time where a piece of art changed your understanding of the medium. A book where you didn't realize writing could be this good. Could be a film that brought you to tears. Maybe a song or album. Typically, we associate this experience with traditional mediums like visual art and music. I’m going to guess you weren’t picturing a video game. I want to discuss a transformative experience I had playing Hollow Knight Silksong, and how it changed my outlook on games as a storytelling device.

Before we get into it, this game is great. Unfortunately, I have to spoil the entire thing to talk about it. So if you’re already thinking about playing, or don’t want to be spoiled, I urge you to click away.

I'm going to assume you have a familiarity with the in-game world and story, for two reasons. First, I won't have to summarize mountains of lore, and second, to incentivize readers who get lost to just stop reading and play the game first. 

The climax of the story's second act is the battle between Hornet and Grand Mother Silk. A normal playthrough will likely result in the same conclusion I reached, which is not a happy ending. In the final scene, Hornet absorbs all the power for herself and takes the throne. We aren’t shown the aftermath, but we’re led to believe one ruler has been replaced with another. It’s unclear if Hornet will be benevolent or just corrupted by power. This ending confused me on my first playthrough. It made my decisions and struggle with the game feel almost futile. In hindsight though, it actually makes a lot of sense to leave the player a little unsatisfied, when you consider the alternate ending in Act 3.

#### Playing Silksong, I was struck by how reluctant the game was to reveal itself. 

Much of the lore and information about the world is quite subtle, and the game makes no attempt to hold your hand. Secrets are so well hidden it sometimes feels like you're discovering something that wasn't meant to be seen. It creates a sense of tension, like the game is trying not to reveal itself to you. This tension is very successful at engaging the player, pulling me in to search deeper, and rewarding me with a more satisfying payoff when I do. 

It’s often to the point that you’re more likely to walk right past something extremely important than you are to actually notice it. For instance, almost any normal looking wall can be breakable, and the visual cues showing pre-existing crack marks are so subtle that they are almost invisible. Sometimes these walls are fully undetectable, obscured by a darkened foreground element. This means the player is almost guaranteed to miss certain rooms, along with lore and items inside. Sometimes its a collectible, worth just a handful of change. But it could be an entire sword upgrade, one of only 3 in the game. This ambiguity in the player's progression was a huge risk the developers took, and its unbelievable how well they pulled it off.

Sometimes this can be frustrating, but I actually prefer it. Whenever I discover a little secret area, I feel like I earned it. It makes the process of discovery and decision making much more empowering. On the whole, the game is stronger for it. All this to say, it makes a sense that the secret ending would be well hidden. 

As you may recall, when you beat the final boss and try to enter back into the game to keep playing, Hornet is placed right before the fight, as if it hasn’t actually happened yet. This was my first clue about Act 3. To me, it reads as though the developers are not content with the player resolving things the way they did. It’s as if they’re hinting that the world has something left to uncover. 

#### This additional storyline is where Silksong ascends from a great game to a masterpiece.

One of the ways Silksong tells its story more effectively is by making comparisons between the virtual and the real world. This game is full of little metaphors, as well as larger narrative themes. Together, everything works as an allegory. And especially the Citadel of Song. In the game, it represents inequality, very similar to what we see in our world. There are two social classes of bugs, separated by status. Essentially, you have the ruling class who don’t have to do any work, and the working class who supports everything. This is what happens to the few pilgrims who actually survive their trek to the Citadel. Just like the player, most will be tricked into falling down an elevator shaft directly into the mines. Here, they’re forced into manual labor to sustain the city’s infrastructure. 

Consider the case of Loam, the treadmill runner in the underworks. He believes that the city is a place of miracles. He hopes that one day, his efforts will be recognized by those above. I felt bad for this guy because he’s very friendly, but he’s just a little misguided. Maybe it was naive, but I was hoping he could come around to see reason. You can imagine my shock when I went back during Act 3 and found him crushed under a fallen rock. It’s a pretty striking example of the Citadel’s true nature.

So maybe the city is not so cool after all. It’s already a pretty good analogy for the inequalities in our own society. But the parallels don't stop there. It also uses religious motives to play a trick on people. The curse promises salvation and eternal forgiveness through work, which is just a cultural myth to keep the bugs busy. My favorite example of this is the confession booth. The confession booth lets you pay 25 rosaries to confess your sins. But once you pay, you get an automated message encouraging you to return to work. 

I feel the need, at this point, to clarify that religion is not frowned upon or criticized in this game. In fact, plenty of religious bugs are shown in a positive light. Usually, it’s when they use it for personal growth or to preserve cultural traditions. The criticism is when religion is used as a tool for corruption. The Citadel creates the narrative that its domination of the world is inevitable and natural. But this is clearly not true. 

Essentially, Silksong is making real world connections using literary devices. This is pretty common with other narrative formats like books. I guess a game can do it too. 

#### But wouldn’t it be crazy if there were other storytelling mechanisms that Silksong employs, like maybe teaching morals and lessons?

Before we get into deeper meanings, we need to understand the normal ending a little better. Throughout her journey, Hornet has helped the bugs she encounters, and she’s improved some of their lives. But there’s usually a twist. For example, she frees the people of Bellhart from the curse of the spider witch, Widow, but she does so to boost her standing among them. She helps the traveling flea caravan locate some of their lost pets, but only at the promise of being rewarded. And in these cases, she’s not addressing the root cause of suffering.

In the ending where Hornet assumes the throne, life doesn’t really change for most bugs. She isn’t required to interact with the world in good faith. But as the game suggests, the story doesn’t have to end here. You can choose to complete additional quests, going above and beyond to help out bugs in various ways. For instance, you can make donations to several town centers. Doing this helps the townspeople fund community projects. Activities like this will eventually reveal the additional storyline. In this quest, once Hornet kills Grand Mother Silk, she doesn’t take the power for herself. Instead, she uses it to end the curse and free all the bugs.

I just want to pause for a moment so we're all on the same page about how novel this is. The third and final act of the story, in which the hero finally makes things right in the world, usurps the evil ruler, and brings justice to the people - the arc in which the moral and narrative themes are finally exposited - is SO obscured that the final credits have literally already rolled by the time you can even begin the quest that sets it in motion. 

#### This is a fundamentally radical way of telling a story, and I love it. 

Imagine if a TV show did something like this, like HBO released a secret additional season of Game of Thrones that only the most worthy among their fans could view, and it elegantly explained everything that happened and tied the whole story up with a nice bow. That's the type of thing we are dealing with here.

As you can tell, this ending is very different from the first. I think it’s because there's a change in Hornet’s character. She’s not just doing good things when it’s convenient for her. She makes things better for everyone, even risking her own life to do so. It's here that some of the broader lessons in Silksong become clear.

Hornet's actions in this chapter are more complete. She combines grassroots community work with top-down systemic change. On one hand, she’s addressing needs through local intervention. But she’s also solving the root cause of the problem. Which of course is the curse. The game argues that social change requires a comprehensive approach and that isolated solutions aren’t really effective.

In literature, there’s actually a word for this. A fictional story that teaches a moral lesson, often featuring talking animals acting like humans. Sounds a lot like Silksong. The word for this is a fable. In this case, the moral lesson is that removing oppressive leaders is not good enough. To really make change you must refuse to take their place.

So how does this connect to video games more broadly? It’s the interaction. What makes this lesson really stick is how the player has agency in the story’s ending. It’s a cooperation between player and character. Hornet’s lack of knowledge about her new environment mirrors the player's own uncertainty about playing the game for the first time. 

In fact, Silksong begins with no instructions, no information, no explicit goals or objectives. So playing is just as much about figuring out what the hell is going on, as it is accomplishing a specific mission. This made the game feel immersive, because I was the one discovering the world. It didn’t just show characters on screen doing the right thing, it actually made me do it for myself. 

#### In the end, it all comes down to the player.

Sure you can take the easy way out, and nothing really changes. But if you dig a little deeper, you get rewarded with a beautiful lesson. So that’s the real distinction with games. It’s a level of engagement that’s not really possible with other formats. And I haven’t forgotten about things like choosing your own adventure books. Technically these are interactive too, but the agency you have over the world is very limited. In games, a decision you made 20 hours ago can affect something right now.

Now I know we’ve only focused on one game in particular here. We talked about all the ways that Silksong tells its story, just the same as any film or book. If you can believe it, I’ve still only covered a little of what Silksong has to offer. Keep in mind, I haven’t touched on the combat, the boss fights, the items and tool system. I haven't even scratched the surface on the game's visual beauty or the way it deliberately parallaxes foreground and background layers as the player moves side to side to communicate visual cues and detail. 

Regardless, Silksong is just one example of a game that can change the perspective of its audience. There are other titles that do the same thing. Games where you have to make difficult decisions that have long term impacts in the world. For instance, The Last of Us, Red Dead Redemption 2, Undertale, Rain World, Disco Elysium, Fallout New Vegas, the full list would go on forever.

I’m almost out of time, so I just want to leave you with a couple things. I hope I’ve convinced you that games are a legitimate artistic medium, that's equally worthy of critical analysis. And second. This one might be the more important takeaway:

#### You should really consider playing Silksong.
